National Indigenous Crops Story Map

Preserving Heritage • Boosting Health • Climate Resilience

Historically, indigenous crops were central to Rwandan diets, naturally adapting to the country’s diverse agro-ecological zones. To explore their potential, this project focuses on four neglected and underutilized species (NUS): Inyabutongo (wild amaranth), Isogo (African nightshade), Isogi (spider plant), and Intagarasoryo (turkey berry). Although these climate-resilient crops require few inputs and are rich in vital nutrients, they are rapidly disappearing from local markets and household meals.

Bringing these crops back alongside the foods Rwanda already grows helps answer key challenges, including climate shocks, reliance on food imports, and diet-related health issues such as child stunting. They add diversity to farms and plates that already rely on staples like maize, rice, beans and bananas. National policy and global frameworks increasingly support NUS, yet these traditional vegetables still need more research and support.

Three simple ways to cook

Quick sauté. Fry chopped onion in a little oil, add tomato, then the greens. Cover and steam for 3–5 minutes.
Greens with groundnut paste. Blanch the leaves for 2 minutes, stir in groundnut paste and simmer 5 minutes. Softens bitterness.
Add to what you already cook. Stir a handful into beans, cabbage or isombe for the last few minutes.

Household cooking ideas, to be reviewed by nutritionists before release.

Common questions

Can I eat it raw? ＋

No. Always cook these leaves and berries.

How do I keep the vitamins? ＋

Cook briefly: steaming or blanching, under 10 minutes, and keep the cooking water.

Why is my harvest small? ＋

Check the garden planner in Module 3: spacing, manure, water, weeding and cutting method all matter.
